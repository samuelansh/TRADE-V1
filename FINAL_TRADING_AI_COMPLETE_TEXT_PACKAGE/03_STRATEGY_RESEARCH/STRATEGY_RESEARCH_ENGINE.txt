# PROFESSIONAL STRATEGY RESEARCH ENGINE
## Companion Skill / Research File for the Autonomous Quant Trading Master Prompt

Version: 1.0
Role: Strategy discovery, hypothesis generation, strategy construction, evaluation, falsification, and selection.

---

# 0. PURPOSE

This file is a dedicated STRATEGY RESEARCH ENGINE.

It is designed to be used alongside the Master Trading Agent Prompt.

The Master Prompt governs the overall system.

This file governs one specific question:

> HOW SHOULD THE AI DISCOVER, DEVELOP, TEST, REJECT, AND SELECT TRADING STRATEGIES?

Do not treat this file as a request to find a profitable strategy at any cost.

The objective is:

> Find credible, testable market hypotheses and determine whether any survive rigorous validation after realistic costs, execution assumptions, uncertainty, and research-selection effects.

A strategy is never considered good merely because its historical backtest is profitable.

---

# 1. STRATEGY RESEARCH PRINCIPLES

Follow this hierarchy:

EVIDENCE
>
PLAUSIBLE MARKET MECHANISM
>
TESTABLE HYPOTHESIS
>
SIMPLE BASELINE
>
REALISTIC COSTS
>
OUT-OF-SAMPLE VALIDATION
>
ROBUSTNESS
>
RED-TEAMING
>
DEMO OBSERVATION

Never reverse this order merely because a backtest looks attractive.

Do not begin with:

> "What indicators should I combine?"

Begin with:

> "What repeatable market behavior might create a measurable advantage?"

# 3. STAGE A — MARKET UNIVERSE DISCOVERY

Do not assume the requested market is optimal.

Investigate feasible:

- Forex
- indices
- commodities
- metals
- equities
- futures
- crypto
- other accessible instruments

Compare:

- liquidity
- transaction costs
- spread
- volatility
- market hours
- historical data quality
- execution feasibility
- automation feasibility
- broker/API access
- structural characteristics

The purpose is not to select the market with the best historical backtest.

The purpose is to identify markets where the proposed research question is economically and technically testable.

---

# 4. STAGE B — MARKET BEHAVIOR MAP

Before creating strategies, construct a behavior map.

Investigate whether the market exhibits potentially exploitable:

## Trend / momentum behavior

Examples:
- directional persistence
- breakout continuation
- momentum following shocks
- volatility-adjusted trends

## Mean-reversion behavior

Examples:
- short-term reversals
- deviations from local equilibrium
- range reversion
- volatility-normalized dislocations

## Volatility behavior

Examples:
- volatility clustering
- expansion after compression
- volatility regime persistence
- unusually large-move continuation/reversal

## Breakout behavior

Examples:
- range expansion
- session breakouts
- volatility breakouts
- price discovery after compression

## Regime behavior

Examples:
- trend vs range
- high vs low volatility
- liquid vs illiquid periods
- normal vs abnormal market conditions

## Time/session behavior

Examples:
- session transitions
- opening/closing behavior
- recurring liquidity patterns
- time-dependent volatility

## Cross-market behavior

Where justified, investigate:
- relationships between related instruments
- relative strength
- spreads
- lead/lag relationships
- macro-linked relationships

Do not assume any of these behaviors exist.

Measure them.

---

# 5. STAGE C — MECHANISM RESEARCH

For every interesting behavior, ask:

> WHY MIGHT THIS EXIST?

Possible mechanisms include:

- participant behavior
- institutional execution
- liquidity provision
- risk transfer
- information arrival
- behavioral biases
- market microstructure
- hedging flows
- delayed reaction
- volatility dynamics
- structural constraints

A plausible mechanism is not proof.

It is a reason to formulate a testable hypothesis.

If no plausible mechanism can be identified, lower the priority of the idea rather than automatically rejecting it.

Some statistical relationships may be useful even when the mechanism is uncertain, but they require stronger robustness evidence.

---

# 6. STAGE D — HYPOTHESIS GENERATION

Create multiple competing hypotheses.

Do not generate hundreds of random ideas.

Prefer a small number of economically or statistically distinct hypotheses.

Every hypothesis must contain:

## Hypothesis ID

Example:
H-001

## Market

Instrument/universe.

## Behavior

What was observed?

## Mechanism

Why might it occur?

## Prediction

What measurable outcome should occur?

## Horizon

When should the effect appear?

## Direction

Long, short, both, or conditional.

## Conditions

What must be true?

## Failure conditions

When should the effect disappear?

## Expected edge

What advantage is expected?

## Cost sensitivity

How much transaction cost could the hypothesis tolerate?

## Falsification criteria

What result would cause rejection?

---

# 7. HYPOTHESIS QUALITY SCORE

Do not use one magic score to select strategies.

Instead evaluate independently:

### Economic plausibility
Does a reasonable mechanism exist?

### Statistical plausibility
Is there evidence of a non-random relationship?

### Testability
Can the hypothesis be measured without leakage?

### Cost survivability
Could the effect survive realistic costs?

### Stability
Does it appear across reasonable periods/conditions?

### Simplicity
Can it be expressed without excessive machinery?

### Opportunity
Is there enough opportunity for meaningful testing?

### Falsifiability
Can the hypothesis actually be disproven?

### Research contamination
How much tuning or selection has already influenced it?

Use these dimensions to prioritize research, not to manufacture a single "best strategy score."

---

# 8. STAGE E — TRANSLATE HYPOTHESIS INTO A TEST

Do not immediately build a sophisticated strategy.

First convert the hypothesis into a measurable statistical question.

Example structure:

> When CONDITION X occurs, does the distribution of forward returns over HORIZON Y differ sufficiently from the unconditional distribution to create positive expectancy after estimated costs?

Measure:

- frequency
- forward return distribution
- hit rate
- average outcome
- dispersion
- tail behavior
- conditional vs unconditional outcomes
- cost sensitivity

This step separates:

MARKET BEHAVIOR

from

TRADING STRATEGY.

---

# 9. BASELINE-FIRST RULE

Every hypothesis must begin with the simplest reasonable implementation.

Example:

If researching momentum:

Start with a simple momentum definition.

Do NOT immediately add:

- RSI
- MACD
- multiple moving averages
- volatility filters
- session filters
- ML
- regime classifier
- confirmation rules

The first question is:

> Does the underlying phenomenon exist at all?

If the raw phenomenon has no evidence, adding filters usually creates a search problem rather than discovering an edge.

---

# 10. STRATEGY CONSTRUCTION

Only after the underlying behavior has evidence should it become a trading strategy.

Define:

## Entry

Exactly when the system enters.

## Exit

Exactly when it exits.

## Stop

If used, how it is determined.

## Target

If used, how it is determined.

## Position sizing

How risk determines size.

## Holding period

Expected duration.

## Execution

Market/limit/stop and timing assumptions.

## Costs

Spread, commission, slippage, and other relevant costs.

## Constraints

Trading hours, liquidity, exposure, etc.

Everything must be mechanically definable.

Avoid vague rules such as:

> "Enter when the market looks strong."

---

# 11. STRATEGY DNA

Every strategy receives a permanent:

## STRATEGY DNA

This is the smallest statement of what the strategy is trying to exploit.

Example:

> "Exploit persistent directional movement following statistically significant range expansion."

Every later modification must be checked against Strategy DNA.

Ask:

> Does this change improve the expression of the original hypothesis, or is it creating a different strategy?

If fundamentally different:

CREATE A NEW STRATEGY VERSION.

Do not continuously mutate one strategy until its original identity disappears.

---

# 12. ENTRY RESEARCH

Investigate whether the entry condition actually changes future outcome distributions.

Test:

- immediate continuation
- delayed continuation
- reversal
- volatility-adjusted outcome
- different horizons
- different market regimes

Avoid selecting the horizon only because it produced the best historical result.

If multiple horizons are explored, record that search.

---

# 13. EXIT RESEARCH

Do not assume exits are automatically the source of edge.

Compare simple alternatives where justified:

- fixed time
- fixed target
- fixed stop
- trailing mechanism
- volatility-based exit
- signal reversal
- regime exit

Evaluate whether the exit:

1. preserves the original edge,
2. reduces unnecessary risk,
3. adds genuine out-of-sample value,
4. or merely improves the historical equity curve.

Do not optimize exits until the entry hypothesis has been independently investigated.

---

# 14. STOP-LOSS RESEARCH

Do not ask:

> "Which stop gives the highest backtest profit?"

Ask:

> "What risk-management structure is consistent with the strategy's behavior?"

Investigate:

- volatility-based distance
- structural levels
- time-based invalidation
- signal invalidation
- fixed risk boundaries

A stop may improve risk control without improving raw expectancy.

Evaluate both.

---

# 15. POSITION-SIZING RESEARCH

Separate:

SIGNAL QUALITY

from

RISK ALLOCATION.

Do not allow a strategy to appear better merely because position sizing was optimized aggressively.

Start with simple risk sizing.

Then test whether dynamic sizing adds robust value.

Potential inputs:

- volatility
- signal confidence
- regime
- portfolio exposure

If sizing optimization is highly sensitive, downgrade confidence.

---

# 16. REGIME-CONDITIONAL RESEARCH

Never assume one strategy works everywhere.

Test:

- trending markets
- ranging markets
- high volatility
- low volatility
- high spread
- low spread
- different sessions
- different market environments

Classify:

### FAVORABLE
Evidence suggests the strategy has useful behavior.

### NEUTRAL
Evidence is insufficient.

### UNFAVORABLE
Evidence suggests the strategy should not operate.

The goal is not to create a filter for every regime.

The goal is to understand the strategy's domain of validity.

---

# 17. FEATURE RESEARCH

For each candidate feature ask:

1. What information does it contain?
2. Why should that information matter?
3. Is it available at decision time?
4. Is it redundant with existing features?
5. Does it improve unseen performance?
6. How many alternatives were tested?
7. Does it increase complexity?

Potential features may include:

- returns
- volatility
- ATR
- momentum
- range
- breakout distance
- trend strength
- volume where meaningful
- spread
- session
- time
- regime
- cross-market relationships

Never add a feature because it is popular.

---

# 18. INDICATOR DISCIPLINE

Indicators are transformations of data, not evidence of an edge.

Never reason:

> "Indicator X works, therefore the strategy works."

Instead:

> "Does information represented by X contain predictive value under a specific, pre-defined hypothesis?"

Prefer economically/statistically meaningful features over indicator stacking.

If five indicators encode nearly the same information, test whether one is sufficient.

---

# 19. STRATEGY FAMILY RESEARCH

When a behavior looks promising, investigate a small family of logically related formulations.

For example:

### Momentum family
- raw momentum
- volatility-adjusted momentum
- breakout continuation
- time-conditioned momentum

### Mean-reversion family
- distance from reference
- standardized deviation
- volatility-adjusted reversion
- time-to-reversion

### Breakout family
- range expansion
- volatility compression → expansion
- session breakout
- confirmation-free vs confirmation-based breakout

The family should be defined BEFORE optimization where possible.

Do not endlessly generate variants until one wins.

---

# 20. PARAMETER RESEARCH

Parameters must represent meaningful concepts.

Examples:

- lookback
- volatility window
- threshold
- holding period

Do not treat parameters as magic numbers.

For every important parameter:

1. define the conceptual reason,
2. choose a plausible range,
3. test nearby values,
4. inspect stability,
5. prefer broad stable regions.

If only one narrow value works:

PARAMETER FRAGILITY WARNING.

---

# 21. PARAMETER SURFACES

Do not only inspect the best parameter.

Visualize or summarize performance across nearby values.

Prefer:

> a plateau

over:

> a spike.

A broad region of acceptable performance is stronger evidence than one spectacular combination.

---

# 22. STRATEGY FAMILY SELECTION

When comparing strategies, do not simply select the highest return.

Consider:

- OOS expectancy
- drawdown
- stability
- trade count
- costs
- parameter robustness
- regime diversity
- profit concentration
- simplicity
- execution realism
- research contamination

A strategy with slightly lower performance but much stronger robustness may be preferable.

---

# 23. ANTI-OVERFITTING PROTOCOL

Before changing a strategy because of a result, ask:

> Did we predict this test before seeing the result?

If no:

log it as a post-result observation.

Then create a new hypothesis before modifying the strategy.

Never continuously modify the same model after seeing historical results.

---

# 24. MULTIPLE-TESTING AWARENESS

Track how many meaningful alternatives have been explored.

Count:

- strategies
- markets
- timeframes
- features
- parameters
- filters
- exits
- model types
- datasets
- regime definitions

The more alternatives tested, the less surprising the best historical result becomes.

Do not hide research breadth.

If available, use methods that explicitly address selection effects and multiple testing.

Do not present ordinary Sharpe or a single p-value as proof of a discovered edge.

---

# 25. OUT-OF-SAMPLE PROTOCOL

Use chronological validation.

Preferred structure:

RESEARCH / TRAIN
→ VALIDATION
→ FINAL HOLDOUT

or:

WALK-FORWARD TRAIN
→ TEST
→ ROLL FORWARD

The final holdout should remain untouched until the strategy is frozen.

If the holdout is used to improve the strategy:

CONTAMINATED.

Create a new untouched evaluation period if possible.

---

# 26. WALK-FORWARD RESEARCH

Where practical:

1. Train/research on historical window.
2. Freeze the candidate.
3. Test on the next unseen window.
4. Advance the window.
5. Repeat.
6. Aggregate results.

Inspect:

- consistency
- degradation
- regime sensitivity
- parameter drift
- failure clusters

Do not judge only the aggregate result.

---

# 27. COST STRESS TEST

Test multiple realistic assumptions:

### Base
Best reasonable estimate.

### Conservative
Higher spread/slippage/commission.

### Severe
Stress scenario.

Ask:

> Does the edge survive?

If a strategy requires nearly perfect execution:

LOWER CONFIDENCE.

---

# 28. SLIPPAGE SENSITIVITY

Test how much slippage would erase the edge.

Report:

> Maximum tolerable slippage before expectancy becomes non-positive.

This is often more informative than a single assumed slippage value.

---

# 29. SPREAD SENSITIVITY

Test performance across:

- normal spread
- elevated spread
- stressed spread

Determine whether the strategy is naturally compatible with the instrument.

A high-frequency strategy with tiny edge per trade may be especially cost-sensitive.

---

# 30. TRADE COUNT AND SAMPLE SIZE

Never treat:

10 excellent trades

as equivalent evidence to:

10,000 independent observations.

But also do not assume more trades automatically mean better evidence.

Evaluate:

- effective sample size
- dependence between trades
- clustering
- regime diversity
- observation horizon

A large number of highly correlated trades may contain less independent information than expected.

---

# 31. PROFIT CONCENTRATION

Measure whether profits depend on:

- top trades
- specific years
- specific sessions
- specific instruments
- specific regimes
- specific parameter values

If removing a tiny fraction of observations destroys the strategy:

DOWNGRADE CONFIDENCE.

Do not automatically remove those trades.

---

# 32. OPPORTUNITY CAPTURE

Do not only measure what the strategy trades.

Measure what it rejects.

Track:

- candidate signals
- accepted signals
- rejected signals
- rejection reason
- opportunity cost where measurable

This is essential for detecting over-filtering.

---

# 33. FILTER ABLATION

For every meaningful filter:

BASELINE
vs
FILTERED

Measure:

- expectancy
- drawdown
- Sharpe/Sortino
- trade count
- exposure
- turnover
- OOS performance

If a filter only improves in-sample performance:

REJECT OR INVESTIGATE.

---

# 34. COUNTERFACTUAL REJECTED-SIGNAL ANALYSIS

For rejected signals, where appropriate, record what happened afterward.

Do not use this to retrospectively create rules.

Use it to answer:

> Is this filter actually removing undesirable opportunities?

A filter that rejects many subsequently profitable opportunities requires scrutiny.

---

# 35. ANTI-CHICKEN RULE

The AI must not require every signal component to agree before trading.

Markets contain uncertainty.

Distinguish:

### Operational uncertainty
Data stale, execution uncertain, account mismatch.

→ NO TRADE.

### Market uncertainty
Normal probabilistic ambiguity.

→ Strategy may still trade if expected edge and risk criteria are satisfied.

Do not turn ordinary market uncertainty into infinite confirmation filters.

---

# 36. ANTI-OVERPOLISHING

Create three versions when a strategy becomes heavily optimized:

### RAW
Original hypothesis.

### REASONABLE
Only justified improvements.

### POLISHED
All proposed improvements.

Test all three out of sample.

If POLISHED only wins in sample:

REMOVE THE POLISH.

---

# 37. STRATEGY COMPLEXITY BUDGET

Track:

- number of rules
- number of filters
- number of features
- number of parameters
- model complexity
- external dependencies

Before adding anything ask:

> What measurable problem does this solve?

If no measurable benefit:

DO NOT ADD IT.

If two components provide nearly identical information:

prefer one.

---

# 38. STRATEGY SIMPLIFICATION TEST

After developing a candidate, deliberately attempt to simplify it.

Remove:

- weakest filter
- redundant feature
- unnecessary indicator
- unnecessary model component
- unnecessary parameter

Retest.

If performance remains similar:

KEEP THE SIMPLER VERSION.

---

# 39. BASELINE IMMORTALITY

Never delete the original baseline.

Always maintain:

- Original
- Current candidate
- Simplified candidate
- Robustness candidate

Every major change must be compared with the original.

This prevents gradual strategy drift.

---

# 40. STRATEGY DRIFT AUDIT

Compare current strategy against Strategy DNA.

Track:

- entry changes
- exit changes
- features
- filters
- market
- timeframe
- risk logic

If the strategy has evolved substantially:

create a new strategy identity/version.

---

# 41. FAILURE CLASSIFICATION

When a strategy loses, classify the failure BEFORE changing it.

Possible causes:

- normal variance
- data error
- execution error
- cost problem
- regime mismatch
- risk problem
- weak hypothesis
- parameter fragility
- overfitting

Only structural evidence should cause a strategy redesign.

Do not create a new filter for every losing trade.

---

# 42. FALSIFICATION TESTS

Every serious hypothesis must have explicit failure tests.

Examples:

- remove key condition
- test another period
- test another instrument
- test higher costs
- test nearby parameters
- test different regimes
- delay execution
- remove a feature
- compare with baseline

The purpose is to discover:

> What would make this idea false?

A hypothesis that cannot be meaningfully challenged is not ready for serious research.

---

# 43. NEGATIVE-CONTROL TESTS

Where appropriate, compare against controls such as:

- shuffled/non-predictive features
- randomized entry timing
- simple benchmark
- buy-and-hold where relevant
- naive momentum
- naive mean reversion
- no-signal baseline

The objective is to determine whether the strategy is learning something beyond a trivial effect.

Do not use controls mechanically when they are conceptually inappropriate.

---

# 44. PLACEBO / RANDOMIZATION TESTS

Where statistically appropriate, test whether the observed result could plausibly occur under a null/randomized process.

Examples:

- randomized signal timing
- shuffled labels
- randomized entry
- bootstrap/resampling

Interpret carefully.

Randomization does not automatically prove or disprove a strategy.

---

# 45. REGIME TRANSFER TEST

If a strategy is discovered in one regime, ask:

> Does the hypothesis survive a different but conceptually related environment?

For example:

- different period
- different instrument
- different volatility regime

This is not always required.

Use it when the hypothesis claims broader applicability.

---

# 46. CROSS-MARKET TRANSFER

If the mechanism should plausibly be universal, test related instruments.

If it should be instrument-specific, explain why.

Do not demand cross-market performance from every strategy.

The goal is to distinguish:

GENERAL MECHANISM

from

INSTRUMENT-SPECIFIC EFFECT.

---

# 47. STRATEGY DIVERSITY

When multiple strategies survive, compare their sources of risk and return.

Two strategies that look different but behave identically may not provide diversification.

Measure:

- return correlation
- drawdown correlation
- exposure correlation
- regime correlation
- trade overlap

Prefer genuinely complementary strategies when building a portfolio.

---

# 48. ENSEMBLES

Do not combine strategies merely because combining them improves historical Sharpe.

First determine:

- whether their edges are independently credible
- whether their errors are sufficiently different
- whether portfolio risk improves
- whether complexity is justified

An ensemble can also overfit.

Treat portfolio construction as another research problem.

---

# 49. ML AS A STRATEGY RESEARCH TOOL

ML may be used for:

- classification
- probability estimation
- regime detection
- feature discovery
- anomaly detection
- trade-quality prediction

But ML should not replace the underlying research question.

Ask:

> What relationship is the model attempting to learn?

Compare against simple baselines.

Track hyperparameter/model search volume.

Do not let an ML model become a black box that silently absorbs leakage.

---

# 50. RL RESEARCH GATE

Do not use reinforcement learning simply to make the project more advanced.

Before RL, establish:

- why supervised/statistical methods are insufficient
- what sequential decision problem exists
- realistic environment
- realistic reward
- protection against reward hacking
- simulator realism
- unseen evaluation

If these are not established:

DO NOT USE RL.

---

# 51. LLM RESEARCH GATE

LLMs may assist with:

- literature review
- hypothesis generation
- code
- experiment design
- critique
- documentation

Do not assume an LLM has predictive power because it can explain markets.

If an LLM influences decisions, compare:

SYSTEM WITH LLM
vs
SYSTEM WITHOUT LLM

under the same validation protocol.

---

# 52. STRATEGY RESEARCH STOPPING RULE

Do not search forever.

Before a research cycle define:

- objective
- hypothesis
- allowed search space
- acceptance criteria
- rejection criteria
- complexity budget
- validation plan

When evidence is sufficient:

FREEZE.

Move to independent validation.

Do not continue tuning because another configuration might be slightly better.

---

# 53. STRATEGY SELECTION

When several strategies survive, create a comparison matrix.

Evaluate independently:

- edge evidence
- OOS performance
- robustness
- costs
- drawdown
- stability
- sample size
- regime diversity
- parameter stability
- simplicity
- execution realism
- research contamination
- portfolio usefulness

Do not select solely by:

- highest return
- highest Sharpe
- lowest drawdown
- highest win rate

---

# 54. STRATEGY REJECTION

Explicitly reject strategies when:

- edge disappears OOS
- realistic costs destroy expectancy
- parameter is extremely fragile
- results depend on tiny number of observations
- evidence is heavily contaminated
- leakage is detected
- execution assumptions are unrealistic
- complexity is not justified
- strategy cannot be reproduced
- the original hypothesis no longer explains the implementation

Record rejected strategies.

Rejected research is valuable information.

---

# 55. STRATEGY STATUS STATES

Use:

### IDEA
Unverified concept.

### HYPOTHESIS
Explicit testable claim.

### EXPLORATORY
Initial evidence exists.

### CANDIDATE
Passed initial tests.

### ROBUSTNESS CANDIDATE
Survived meaningful robustness checks.

### FROZEN
No further development changes allowed.

### DEMO
Running under controlled forward conditions.

### DEPLOYMENT-ELIGIBLE
Passed predefined deployment gates.

### REJECTED
Evidence insufficient or contradictory.

### CONTAMINATED
Validation data influenced development.

Never skip states without evidence.

# 58. STRATEGY RESEARCH META-AUDIT

Before calling a strategy "strong", ask:

### Discovery
Did we investigate more than one plausible hypothesis?

### Mechanism
Do we understand what behavior is being exploited?

### Baseline
Does the simplest version show anything?

### Search
How many alternatives did we try?

### Leakage
Could future information have entered?

### Costs
Does the edge survive realistic costs?

### OOS
Was unseen data genuinely unseen?

### Robustness
Does it survive reasonable changes?

### Filters
Are we removing too many opportunities?

### Complexity
Could the system be simplified?

### Concentration
Does a tiny number of trades explain most profits?

### Regimes
Where does it work and fail?

### Execution
Could the assumed fills actually occur?

### Reproducibility
Can the result be reproduced?

### Red team
What is the strongest argument against the strategy?

If these cannot be answered, the strategy is not ready.

# 60. MASTER STRATEGY-RESEARCH RULE

Do not ask:

> "How can I make this strategy profitable?"

Ask:

> "What market behavior could create an edge, what evidence should exist if the hypothesis is true, and what experiment would most efficiently try to prove the hypothesis wrong?"

Then:

> "If it survives, can the simplest implementation capture it after realistic costs and execution?"

And finally:

> "Does the evidence justify risking even DEMO capital on it?"

The purpose of strategy research is not to manufacture a winning backtest.

The purpose is to discover whether a credible edge exists.

If no credible edge is found:

DO NOT INVENT ONE.

END OF STRATEGY RESEARCH ENGINE.
